academia has always been political, yet we cling to the myth of neutrality — and that myth is harming us. my bachelor's thesis opens with a quote on its first page:
<br>

<center><a href=" https://www.uncommonengineer.com/blog/technology-political-no-matter-how-much-people-try/">But the truth of the matter is,<br> technology is very much political - <br>in how it's created, developed, <br>by whom, for whom, and why?</a></center>
<br>

i put it there <b>expecting</b> somebody to point it out. all i got was a "..are you sure you want to keep that?" by my supervisor, to whom i replied "well, yes i am prepared for criticism" and he was satisfied by that.

during the last year i have been graced with experimenting how much of a stick-up-the-ass engineering faculty members are- which should not come as a surprise but goddamn. i have been told <i>"our kind doesnt do politics. you can't ask these people to care about such things, they just want to do research and study"</i> by somebody who is fully aware of their position, political influence and of course. is an engineer themselves.
this place has time and time again said in the smallest possible voice that their technology is for the people, it is being developed for good and everyone here is a good person, "you're just in mala fede". hell yeah i am, military funding dresses well, and we have been looking away for so long we have become blind.

<h2>historically, universities were never neutral spaces</h2>

they emerged from cathedral and monastic schools — institutions whose entire job was training the Church's bureaucrats and theologians, not liberating anybody. explicitly Christian establishments, training clergy and administrators of the Church: theology, canon law, liturgy, but also logic, rhetoric, and basic "management" of Church property and finances.

universities didn't spring up as some pure space of knowledge; they were corporate bodies negotiating privileges with popes, emperors, and city governments, producing lawyers and clerics for those same powers.

universities used to be the places where people could flee to. that could be a point in favor to the "neutral" argument... if we were to stop here. it would be as neutral as priests and monks sheltering fugitives, the disabled, the unfortunate or the poor. and i'm not saying that to give the Church credit, it's a fucked up entity that should be dismantled by all means. charity from a power structure isn't neutrality, it's maintenance.

<h2>local communities and small towns</h2>

favored ~(still do)~ the rich and powerful but resistance was always there, and its connecting value was solidarity — people looking out for each other from the bottom, not power looking down and deciding who deserved shelter.

the same went for universities. people from every background shared the desire to study the world — the rich already had private tutors, they didn't need the institution for that. it was ordinary people who wanted in. but wanting in and being let in are not the same thing: the desire was universal, the door wasn't.

it stayed shut to the very people building the cathedrals, working the fields, and funding the whole enterprise through tithes and taxes — _the institution was never built for them, only occasionally merciful to a few of them_.
and even where you did find something like solidarity inside universities, it's not the kind i meant earlier. students banded together to defend their own interests against city authorities, landlords, whoever tried to squeeze them. call it a proto class struggle if you want, but it was a battle fought entirely within a narrow, already-educated stratum. it's not the same solidarity as a town looking out for its poor. it's a guild protecting its own.

<blockquote>universities were never "for everyone"; they were instruments for reproducing a ruling class. </blockquote>

you needed to already know someone, some patron or bishop or family connection; strip away any one of those and you weren't getting in, full stop. so when people say "universal," universal to who? it was universal in the sense that Latin was a common language across borders but that's a universality for the elite, a passport that worked across the whole map of people who already had passports.

<blockquote>this is where the class struggle comes in. "universal" in universitas never meant universal. in order to even get in, you needed Latin! access has always been filtered by geography, language, and cultural capital; if you did not have money and wealth you could hope for the Church to decide you were worth the investment. even now, when we pretend "everyone can study," the institution quietly sorts people by who can afford rent, who can survive unpaid internships, who can navigate bureaucracy, who already speaks the right academic dialect.</blockquote>

<h2>and here's the thing</h2>

if that's what a university is, historically, structurally, then what it should be starts to look very different from "excellence" or "employability" or whatever the KPI of the month is. it should be a place of systematic dissent. a community of people who are, quite literally, paid to question what we think we already know. not to produce workers. not to produce rankings. to produce doubt, on purpose, as a public good.

<blockquote><b>neutrality is a myth that hides the fact that universities have always been political tools</b>, shaped by the interests of those who fund them — whether that's the medieval Church or today's state, corporations, and yes, the military.</blockquote>

Giorgio La Pira^[2](https://documenti.camera.it/_dati/costituente/lavori/I_Sottocommissione/sed050/sed050.pdf) put it better than i can: a professor holds no executive power, no judicial power — only <i>il potere di dire la verità secondo la sua coscienza</i>, the power to speak the truth according to their own conscience.
and this is exactly why, when a government goes after university autonomy it isn't a side quest. an authoritarian power is not scared of universities because they're inefficient or elitist or full of old men in ridiculous robes. it's scared of them because, when they actually work, <b>they produce something a government can't buy or manage</b>: free minds. people who can't be moved by the news cycle. that's the real target.^[1](https://www.einaudi.it/catalogo-libri/problemi-contemporanei/libera-universita-tomaso-montanari-9788806267667/)
